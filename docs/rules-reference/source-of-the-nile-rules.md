## Introduction

The source of the River Nile was a mystery older than civilization. To the ancient Egyptians, the life-giving, annually-flooding, changeless and everchanging river was a sacred thing. Surely, through the centuries, nameless thousands must have set out up the river to find the mystic land from whence its endless waters flowed.

But their path was hard. Five hundred miles above Thebes they would have encountered the first of six great cataracts, rapids whose thundering, swirling waters made further progress by boat impossible. For those who continued their journey on foot, the land beyond the cataracts was still more inholespitable. The Sudan, with its searing heat, swarming insects, pestilence and savage tribes discouraged all but the most resolute exploration.

Midway through the gauntlet of the Sudan, at Khartoum, the river forked. The Blue Nile, coming from the southeast, could be followed to Belfodiyo where it plunged from the Abbysinian plateau in a series of cataracts through mountain gorges so rugged as to defy even 20th-Century exploration. The White Nile, southwest of Khartoum, could be ascended more easily until it became lost and stagnant among an endless swampland called the Sudd.

These, then, were the ancient limits of the search for the source of the Nile. If any exceptionally hardy and daring explorers did push their way beyond the marshes or the mountains—and we know today that some must have done so—the discoveries they made were dismissed as fantasy.

During the Nineteenth Century, however, Europe was, for a time, freed from the scourge of war that had so preoccupied it since the Fall of Rome. The abilities and ambitions of Western Civilization were turned to better ends for five generations that made European culture and influence predominant throughout the world. Prominent among the many challenges taken up by these generations of peace was the search for the Nile.

However, the source of the Nile was not the only point of geographic interest. The discovery of the source of any of the great rivers which reached the African coast was greeted with acclaim. In fact, had the Congo or the Niger proved to be a longer river than the Nile, the more enduring fame might have gone to its explorers. Other outstanding features—great lakes, major waterfalls, and majestic mountain peaks—were milestones on the path of exploration.

Men of less noble motive sought the sources of gold, diamonds, ivory and furs that Africa had sparingly given up for centuries. Others sought land, water and wood for farms and homes, or iron and coal for industry. To the zoologist the teeming, exotic variety of African flora and fauna might hold the secrets of life. To the doctor, the equally exotic and terrible African diseases offered the challenge of death. To missionaries the African peoples were souls lost in a darkness of ignorance and superstition. To the early anthropologists they were primitive peoples whose "simple" tribal customs were to be studied as curiosities. To the journalist, they were the victims of brutal and barbarous slave trade, which he successfully brought to an end.

## Description of Game

**Source of The Nile** is designed to be an educational and challenging game in which players compete primarily with Nature and only incidentally with each other. Luck tends to be a major element in the game but can be overcome by careful play. Because the game situation is forever changing, there is no single best strategy; however, players must weigh risks against gains with every decision they make and good judgment will pay off.

The game is played on a map-board showing Africa from Khartoum to Capetown and Lagos to Mombasa. The periphery of this map, showing the territory known ca. 1820, is filled-in; the center is blank. An hexagonal grid is superimposed on the map to regulate movement and to delimit territory explored. In the course of play, each player moves a token (representing his explorer) from explored hexagons onto blank hexagons one at a time; terrain, native tribes and other discoveries within the hexagon are then semi-randomly determined, with allowance made for previous discoveries in adjacent hexagons. The player interacts with the discoveries (e.g., negotiates with new natives for guides) and records them on the mapboard using wax crayons. The mapboard surface is erasable; if the explorer dies before returning to Europe to publish his discoveries, all hexagons which only he has explored will be erased and "subsequent explorers will find that his discoveries were only rumors"; i.e., when other players explore the erased hexagons later they will have to generate new discoveries there.

When explorers do return to civilization, however, they may publish their discoveries, after which they are safe from erasure. Players may also emulate real explorers by withholding some of their discoveries since they will enjoy some advantages when moving through terrain or dealing with natives known only to them. Points are scored for discoveries as they are published and once scored will not be lost if the explorer dies or is retired by his player. A player may retire his explorer at any time and then start a new explorer in Europe. Thus each player may operate several explorers in one game, although no player may operate more than one explorer at a time.

Before each trip to Africa, explorers will have to raise money to support their expedition. New explorers with no discoveries to their credit must do this by drawing chance cards for several turns until they feel their finances are adequate. Experienced explorers can accelerate this process by publishing discoveries, which will allow them to draw additional chance cards.

As there are 452 blank hexagons to be explored, the game is not usually played to the bitter end at one sitting. Instead a reasonable time-limit is set, at the end of which all explorers retire and the player with the most points is the winner. The complete exploration of Africa—and the final determination of the source of the Nile—can be the result of several games, each starting where the last left off. After all, the adventure is in the search for the source of the Nile, not in the finding of it!

## Starting the Game

Players lay out the board and sort the counters into one color group for each player (white counters are native tribes). Each player also receives an Expedition Organization Chart and designates whether his first explorer will be a missionary-, doctor-, zoologist-, geologist-, or ordinary explorer (see *Explorer Specialties* below). Players roll four dice apiece; high man then becomes the first to play, and play continues clockwise around the board.

Explorers start in Europe (off the board) and return there from time to time to publish their discoveries. At the start of the game, each player is dealt five cards representing donations received by his explorer towards his next expedition to Africa. Explorers returning from Africa will be allowed to draw one card plus additional cards every time they publish hexes which they have explored (see *Publication*).

Most donations are money ($5 to $500) but some are free tickets to Africa. Free tickets must be used or discarded in the next turn—they cannot be saved. Every turn that the explorer remains in Europe he will first discard any free tickets that he holds, discard additional cards, if necessary, to reduce his hand to five, and draw one card. He may then publish any previously unpublished hexes, drawing one card for each hex. Whenever the supply of cards is exhausted, the discards are shuffled together and added to the bottom of the deck.

The cards drawn by each player are placed face up before him—there is no secrecy in the donations received.

## Getting to Africa

When the player decides that his explorer has raised enough money for an expedition, he skips one turn while moving the explorer marker of his color to one of the African Ports of Entry: **Khartoum, Mombasa, Zanzibar, Kilwa, Quilimane, Lourenco Marques, Durban, Port Elizabeth, Capetown, Benguela, Luanda, Calabar, or Brass**. While waiting for his next turn, he organizes his expedition; hiring natives as bearers, guides, or askaris, and buying gifts, rations, camels, horses, canoes or muskets using the Expedition Price List, up to the limit of his budget. He must also pay $500 for his passage to Africa unless he has a "free ticket" card and the port of entry he has chosen. Players should spend as much money on their expeditions as possible to improve their chances of success; any money not spent on the expedition is lost.

*Special Note on Zanzibar.* Although Zanzibar is on an island in the Indian Ocean, it is not necessary for an expedition starting there to buy canoes in order to reach the mainland of Africa. Treat movement from Zanzibar to either of the adjacent coastal veldt hexes as normal, overland movement through known terrain.

## Expedition Organization

Players hire or buy the following at ports of entry or friendly tribes:

*   **Guides** — $50 each
*   **Bearers** — $10 each
*   **Askaris** — $50 each
*   **Camels** — $50 each (available only at Khartoum)
*   **Horses** — $50 each (available only at Capetown, Durban or Port Elizabeth)
*   **Canoes** — $50 each
*   **Muskets** — $10 each
*   **Food** — $2 each
*   **Gifts** — $2 each

### Rules of Personnel and Equipment:

*   **Bearers** will not hunt, fight or guide. They carry food, water, and gifts, paddle or carry canoes, and lead camels or horses, if one has them. Each bearer may carry 10 rations of food, or two rations of food and two of water, or ten gifts or muskets. Eight bearers may carry one canoe. One bearer can lead three camels or horses.
*   **Guides** will not hunt, fight, paddle or carry. They will reduce one's chances of getting lost; a guide can never get lost in his home hex (the one in which he was hired), and will help the explorer to avoid becoming lost when in hexes adjacent to home.
*   **Askaris** will not guide, carry, or paddle. They will hunt and fight. Food obtained by hunting can greatly extend one's food supply. When hostile natives or dangerous animals are encountered, askaris can often save one's life!
*   **Camels** are very useful in the desert hexes. They must be led or ridden. Bearers may lead up to three camels each, and may ride or walk. Askaris and guides will ride, but not lead, camels. A led camel can carry 20 rations of food or the equivalent weight of muskets, gifts, or water rations. A ridden camel can carry a rider and five rations or the equivalent in gifts, etc.
*   **Horses**, while not so useful in desert as camels, can be used in jungle. Otherwise, the same restrictions on use and capacity apply.
*   **Canoes** are useful in movement along rivers, across lakes, and through swamps. A canoe can carry twelve men (including paddlers) and unlimited food, gifts, and muskets. It cannot carry camels or horses. Bearers are needed to paddle and carry canoes (See *Movement*).
*   **Muskets** can be bought (at ports only) for use as gifts. They can also be used in hiring friendly natives as askaris once one is inland. Each musket weighs the same as one food ration.
*   **Food** consists of rations of salt meat, biscuit, etc. Each man in the expedition consumes one ration each turn. In some regions, explorers can count on hunting for food; in others, they must carry everything they will eat. In desert hexes, horses consume four rations per turn per horse. If shot, camels or horses can be eaten by men, but not by camels or horses. Each camel or horse provides ten rations.
*   **Water** is always available except in desert hexes without oases or rivers. When going into such a desert hex, one must take water rations (obtained at no cost in any hex containing water). Each water ration weighs as much as four food rations. Horses consume four water rations every turn. Men consume one water ration every turn. Camels may consume either four water rations every turn or eight water rations every other turn. *Note that explorers in the desert have been known to shoot thirsty camels or horses, but that shooting thirsty bearers or askaris to save water is not allowed!*
*   **Gifts** are used as presents to natives; to barter for food, horses, camels, or canoes; and to hire native guides, bearers, and askaris. Each gift weighs as much as one food ration.
*   **Explorer Respect**: Explorers can do anything an askari or bearer can do, but when an explorer does menial "bearer" work (carrying, paddling, or leading pack animals), he loses the respect of his askaris, who will desert at a rate of one per turn. Askaris will continue to desert until the explorer gains a victory over hostile natives or, if a suitable disaster card happens to be drawn, kills a dangerous animal.
*   **Caches**: Expeditions can be reorganized on any turn: explorers can cache muskets, canoes, rations, or gifts; turn loose (or eat) horses or camels; or dismiss guides, bearers, or askaris whenever they want to do so. For example, an explorer who has been following a river upstream in canoes and finds his path blocked by a jungle-swamp hex may decide to abandon his canoes and continue to explore on foot. Abandoned animals or men will simply disappear (even if one leaves guards on them). Canoes, rations, gifts or muskets may be cached (buried or hidden) by placing one of the explorer's cache markers on the hex and making a record of its contents. If the explorer ever returns to the hex, roll one die: on a 1 or 2 the cache has disappeared. Explorers can only find other explorers' caches published in published hexes. Expeditions can also gain new men, animals, or equipment by trading with natives encountered.
*   **Expedition Organization Charts** are provided as playing aids. The number of bearers, askaris, etc., in a player's expedition are recorded by placing the appropriate kind of counter on the three numbered tracks. For example, the explorer taking 135 rations and 75 gifts on his expedition will place ration markers on the boxes marked 100, 30 and 5, and gift markers on boxes marked 70 and 5. The markers on the chart are moved to reflect changes in the composition of the expedition. The markers for bearers, askaris, etc., are only used on the Expedition Organization Chart—they are not placed on the mapboard. Only the explorer and cache markers are used on the mapboard.

## Joint Expeditions

Two or more players may send their explorers on a joint expedition. They may pool their donations but free tickets may only be used by the player who has drawn them. The joint expedition will move only once, on the turn of the last of the players to move, each round. Expeditions that have started separately which eventually reach the same hex may find each other and join forces if both players wish to do so and neither is following policy 1 or 2 towards natives in the hex. Joint expeditions may split up whenever any of the players desires to move away independently. If players cannot agree on the division of the expedition's assets, roll one die per explorer for each canoe etc. in dispute and give it to the high roller.

Joint expeditions will check only once each turn for becoming lost and will become lost (or not) as a group. If a joint expedition is defeated by natives each explorer will consult the consequences of defeat table for himself and each will move independently on the next turn. If disaster cards cause an explorer to suffer an accident, roll one die for each explorer and inflict bad luck on the low roller.

Discoveries made by joint expeditions may not be shared; each hex that is explored may be published by only one explorer and his player will receive all points for its contents.

## Order of a Turn (in Africa)

An explorer may do either Step 2 (Movement/Mapping) OR Step 2 (Explorer Specialties) in a single turn, but NOT both.

1.  **Disasters** (if not in Port of Entry or Cape Colony)
2.  **Exploration of New Hexes** (Movement, Becoming Lost, Mapping Blank Hexes, Discovery of Natives) **OR Explorer Specialties** (Geology, Zoology, Medicine, or Evangelism)
3.  **Interaction with Natives** (if any)
    *   Explorer's Policy towards Natives
    *   Natives' Reaction to Explorer's Policy
    *   Victory, Defeat, or Negotiations and Trading (if desired)
4.  **Hunting and Adjustment of Rations**
5.  **Starvation, Sickness and Desertion** (as appropriate)

## Disasters

The card deck used to obtain donations when in Europe is also used for Disasters (and other strange happenings) in Africa. Each card bears two messages—that in the lower right hand corner being for donations, while the disasters section is in the upper left. Only one of these messages applies when the card is drawn, depending on whether the Explorer is in Europe or in Africa. Every turn that the Explorer is in Africa, but not in a Port of Entry or Cape Colony, he will draw one of these cards for a possible disaster. If the message is not applicable to his circumstances (e.g., he is in a desert hex and draws "Canoe overturns in a storm on the lake...") there will be no effect. Otherwise he must roll for damage or suffer the losses indicated by the card. The card is then discarded.

Sometimes the disasters can be averted:
*   **Animal attacks** can be prevented by Askaris. Roll one die per Explorer and Askari: one animal is killed for each 6 rolled. Zoologist-explorers will kill on a roll of 5 or 6.
*   **Sickness** may be treated by doctor-explorers. They can also prevent it. Roll one die: on a six the doctor's strict sanitation measures have kept his men from picking up the disease, or his skill has saved a man from poisoning, snakebite, etc.
*   **Taboos and magic** can be overcome by missionary-explorers. If he is willing to spend one turn without moving, a Missionary-Explorer can preach to his men. Roll 1 die, subtract 2 for effect (-2 to +4) on their attitude which is initially zero. When their attitude is greater than +5 they will ignore "bad omens" and "magic" types of disaster cards. For attitudes of +1 to +5 roll one die: they will ignore the card if the result is less than or equal to their attitude.
*   **Geologist-explorers** can spot potential rockslides and flash floods and will be safe from them.

*Note on Personnel Sickness/Death:* When canoes overturn or are sunk, all contents except the men are certainly lost. When only some of the canoes in an expedition are lost, the specific canoes lost will be determined by numbering the canoes and rolling a die. The number of men drowned is determined by rolling two dice. Overturned canoes are not lost, sunk canoes are.

When a disaster card causes guides (or bearers, or askaris) to become sick or be killed, and the expedition has no men of that type, the Explorer is affected instead! This makes it worthwhile to hire a few guides, bearers or askaris for insurance, whether one needs their useful services or not!

### Cape Colony
Cape Colony is defined as all published and pre-printed hexes along the Orange and Vaal Rivers and their tributaries, and all hexes south of the Orange. The Cape Colony ports of entry are Capetown, Port Elizabeth, and Durban. The Advantages of being in Cape Colony are:
1.  No disaster cards are drawn.
2.  The expedition will not starve, although if an explorer is already sick when he enters Cape Colony, he may die of disease.

## Exploring: Movement

Once the explorer has reached Africa and organized his expedition he will set out to explore the blank and unpublished hexes of the mapboard. He may explore one or two new hexes each turn (depending on his rate of movement).

*   **On foot**: The explorer will move 2 hexes per turn in areas the explorer has previously passed through, areas known at the start of the game, or areas which have been published. These are hereafter known as "known hexes." In "unknown hexes" (not previously visited by that explorer, etc.), the expedition will move only one hex per turn. Expeditions on foot cannot move through lake or swamp hexes.
*   **Riding horses or camels**: The expedition will move four hexes per turn in known areas; or it may move through two known hexes and attempt to explore a third, unknown, hex; or it may attempt to explore two unknown hexes in succession. In the last case, if it becomes "lost" when attempting to explore the first unknown hex that turn, the expedition is not allowed to explore further. Horses may be purchased at Capetown, Port Elizabeth or Durban and may move into any terrain except swamp or lake. Camels may be purchased at Khartoum or from Desert Tribes and may move into any terrain except jungle, swamp, or lake.
*   **Moving by canoe**: The expedition may move up to four hexes through known areas, and two hexes per turn through unknown areas, provided at least eight bearers are paddling each canoe. For four to seven bearers, the top speed in known areas is only three hexes per turn; for two or three bearers it is two and for one bearer it is one hex per turn. In unknown areas, two to seven paddlers can move the canoe at one hex per turn. Even without paddlers, a canoe can always move one hex per turn down a river, drifting with the current. If all the canoes in an expedition do not have the same number of paddlers, the expedition will move at the speed of the slowest canoe. Canoes may not enter jungle-swamp hexes. Canoes may move along rivers running through any type of terrain, follow the coastline of Africa and cross lake hexes at the above speeds. Canoes may also move through swamp hexes, but at half the above speeds, requiring at least eight paddlers to enter an unknown swamp hex. Canoes may be carried into hexes that do not contain lake, river, or swamp at normal "on foot" speeds by eight bearers per canoe (these bearers cannot carry any other loads). When an expedition travelling by canoe reaches a cataract, it cannot move its canoes past the obstacle unless there are at least eight bearers left in the expedition. Canoes may only be paddled or carried by bearers, as these activities are beneath the dignity of askaris and guides. Canoes may be purchased at any port of entry.

*Expedition Speed Limit:* Expeditions which do not have enough canoes, horses, or camels to carry all men and supplies will move at the "on foot" rate.

*Terrain Conflict:* When an expedition moves into a previously unmapped hex and discovers it to contain a type of terrain through which it normally could not move (e.g. a camel-mounted expedition finds a swamp hex), the expedition must go back to the hex from which it came (at the end of the turn). It cannot return to the impassable hex unless it reorganizes to eliminate the conflict. However, the explorer can get credit for the newly-mapped hex even if he does not re-enter the hex.

## Exploring: Becoming Lost

Whenever an explorer attempts to enter an unknown hex, he may become lost. When this happens, he and his expedition will remain in the hex in which he started for the rest of the turn. The likelihood of this happening depends on several factors, resolved in four steps:

*   **Step 1. Terrain:**
    *   A. If following a river downstream, an explorer will not become lost.
    *   B. If following a river upstream, the player will roll one die and subtract one from the result, then go to Step 2.
    *   C. If moving out of a lake, desert, or veldt hex and not following a river, roll one die and subtract one, then go to Step 2.
    *   D. If moving out of a hex containing swamp or mountains and not following a river, roll one die and subtract two, then go to Step 2.
    *   E. If moving out of a hex containing jungle and not following a river, roll one die and subtract three (if the hex also contains swamp or mountains subtract five), then go to Step 2.
*   **Step 2. Guides:**
    *   A. If the explorer is traveling with a guide hired in the hex which he is attempting to leave, he will not become lost.
    *   B. If the explorer is traveling with a guide hired in a hex adjacent to the hex he is attempting to leave, add one to the modified die roll result from Step 1, then go to Step 3.
*   **Step 3. Illness:**
    *   If the explorer is sick, wounded, or starving, subtract one from the modified die roll result from Steps 1 and 2, then go on to Step 4.
*   **Step 4. Resolution:**
    *   If the modified die roll result from Steps 1, 2, and 3 is less than or equal to zero, the explorer becomes lost. Otherwise, he enters the unknown hex he was trying to reach.

## Exploring: Mapping

When an explorer succeeds in reaching a blank, unmapped hex, he will determine its terrain features and draw them onto the mapboard using the appropriately colored wax crayons. There are six types of basic terrain, each of which is represented by a colored symbol: **desert, jungle, lake, swamp, veldt (grassland), and mountain**. Jungle terrain may also be found in combination with Swamp or Mountain; otherwise the basic terrain types are exclusive and cannot be mixed together in the same hex.

### Basic Terrain Generation
There is a good chance that the basic terrain in the blank hex will prove to be the same as that in an adjacent hex that has already been explored. Roll one die and consult the directional pattern printed on the mapboard. If the hex adjacent to the blank hex in the direction indicated by the roll is not blank, then the basic terrain in the blank hex being mapped will be the same. If the hex indicated is blank, roll again. If the hex indicated by the second roll is also blank, roll one six-sided die for random terrain as follows, to obtain the basic terrain for the hex being mapped:

*   **1** = Desert (see Note A)
*   **2** = Mountain
*   **3** = Swamp
*   **4** = Jungle (see Note B)
*   **5** = Veldt
*   **6** = Lake

*Note A:* If any adjacent hex is already known to contain jungle, this will yield veldt rather than desert.  
*Note B:* If any adjacent hex is already known to contain desert, this will yield veldt rather than jungle.

If the random terrain generated is jungle, roll the die again to see if the hex also contains swamp (roll of 3) or mountain (roll of 2); other terrain types resulting from this second roll are ignored. Likewise, if the random terrain generated has been swamp or mountain, roll the die to see if the hex is also jungle (roll of 4), ignoring other results. (If a hex adjacent to a random swamp or mountain hex is known to contain desert, do not roll for jungle).

### Rivers and Oases
After basic terrain has been determined, the explorer must check for the presence of rivers or oases. If the new hex being mapped is **LAKE, SWAMP, or JUNGLE-SWAMP**, there will be no visible river course through the hex (skip ahead to *Marking Explored Hexes*). 

For other basic terrain types, the presence or absence of rivers in the new hex is largely decided by the terrain in adjacent hexes which have already been explored, especially when there are rivers in these hexes. There are three general cases:

#### 1. River or Rivers Known to Flow OUT of the New Hex
If more than one river is known to flow out of the new hex, it must be a lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp: erase any other basic terrain already determined to be there and roll a die for random terrain to decide which of the three types it is.

When only one river is known to flow out of a new hex, it may end there, or be connected to rivers, swamps, or lakes in other adjacent hexes. If one or more other rivers are also known to flow into the new hex, all of the rivers are connected. If there are no other rivers known to flow into the new hex, but there are unexplored hexes (or lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp hexes not already connected to the river) which are adjacent to the new hex, the river will usually be connected to one of those hexes. Roll one die and consult the directional indicator printed on the mapboard. If the hex lying in the direction indicated by the die roll is a lake, swamp, jungle-swamp, or unexplored hex, the river will flow out of it to connect with the part flowing out of the new hex being mapped. If the hex in the indicated direction is some other terrain type—or if the river is already somehow connected to that lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp through rivers, lakes, swamps, and/or jungle-swamps in other hexes (which would imply that it flows uphill somewhere), the river ends. 

If ending is prohibited by the *Drainage Basin Rule*, the river branches into two parts, either of which may end (but not both) or be connected to an adjacent hex; repeat the process for each branch. If there is only one possible hex to which the river could be connected, and if it cannot end (due to the Drainage Basin Rule), then the river will be connected to that hex and no die rolling is necessary. When a river branches and two branches are then found to be connected to the same adjacent hex, they simply rejoin into one branch (running around either side of a large island). If there are no adjacent hexes to which the river would be connected, it will end in the center of the new hex, regardless of the Drainage Basin Rule. Mark the end with a blue dot.

#### 2. River or Rivers Known to Flow INTO the New Hex
A river that is known to flow into a new hex is only going to end there if there is nowhere else to go—all other hexes have been explored and none of them are swamps, lakes or jungle-swamps, or have rivers running out through them from the new hex. In that case, the entire hex will be a great salt lake (this replaces any other terrain that has been found there). If there are one or more unexplored hexes (or explored lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp hexes not already connected to the river) which are adjacent to the new hex, the problem is easily solved. Roll 1 die and consult the directional indicator. If the hex lying in that direction from the new hex is lake, swamp, jungle-swamp, or unexplored, the river will cross the new hex to reach it. If the hex in the indicated direction is an explored hex containing some other terrain type—or if the river is already somehow connected to that lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp (which would imply that it is flowing uphill) roll again until a permissible hex is obtained. If several rivers flow into a hex, they will join together there to form one river which will then flow out as above, or form a salt lake if there is no possible exit.

#### 3. No Rivers Known to Flow Into or Out of the New Hex
Roll 1 die. If the result is odd, there definitely will not be any rivers in the new hex. If the result is even, there may be a river source in the hex. Roll the die again and consult the directional indicator. If the adjacent hex in the indicated direction is lake, swamp, jungle-swamp, or unexplored, a river source is located at the center of the hex (mark it with a blue dot) and a river flows from there into the adjacent hex indicated. If the adjacent hex indicated is not one of the four types given above, there will be no river source or river in the new hex.

*Oasis Finding:* River sources may not be found in DESERT hexes. Instead, when one rolls a source by the above procedure, one finds an **OASIS**. This is the only way to find an oasis. Desert hexes only have rivers running through them to or from adjacent, previously-explored hexes.

Unlike other terrain features, which will be the same for any explorer who enters an unpublished hex that has already been mapped by someone else (unless the first explorer dies before the second one arrives), OASES are only certain for those who find them! The first time each explorer enters an unpublished desert hex without a river, he will check for an Oasis. If he is unsuccessful, he may only try on subsequent turns if the first explorer to enter the hex found an oasis (on his first attempt, obviously). Once the hex is published, the whole world will know where its oasis is (if there is one)—but until then, only explorers who have found it the hard way can use it.

Before looking for an oasis, the explorer must pick up one water ration per man and four per horse or camel in the hex he is leaving. He had better carry one per man and four per horse with him on his search, for, if he does not find an oasis, his men and horses will have to drink that much or die (see *Starvation and Sickness*); his camels can move another hex (back to where he started, or off into the unknown, if he wants to risk it!) before they have to drink again.

### Drainage Basin Rule
The approximate size of the rivers pre-printed on the mapboard was estimated by early explorers from the volume of water discharged at their mouths. The Congo, for example, was almost certain to be a longer river than the Vaal. To reflect this knowledge, a number is printed at the mouth of each river; there should be at least this many hexes of river, swamp, jungle-swamp, or lake connected to the mouth of the river. Until this condition has been satisfied, the river can only end in a new hex if there is no adjacent hex to which it can be joined. However, if a river has branched, either branch may end as long as the other can continue.

*Special Notes on Lake Hexes:* Two lakes which are already joined by a river (which implies that one lake is higher than the other) cannot also be joined by a lake hex (which implies that the two lakes are merely parts of the same lake, and thus at the same level). If one discovers a lake hex that would connect two such lakes, replace it with a swamp hex.

If a river has not yet satisfied the Drainage Basin Rule and all of its branches have ended in lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp hexes—or if a river is known to flow into a lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp out of which no rivers are known to flow—and if there are one to six unexplored hexes adjacent to the lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp hexes involved, then the river must continue across one of these unexplored hexes. (If there are no unexplored hexes available, the river system must end, the Drainage Basin Rule notwithstanding; if there are more than six unexplored hexes available they should all be explored until only six are left.) Whenever an explorer enters one of the six unexplored hexes and checks for rivers, he will treat the case as though a river was known to flow between the new hex and the adjacent lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp (unless the new hex also happens to be a lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp).

Lake hexes which do not have rivers flowing into them may be completely landlocked, drained and fed by underground springs.

### Cataracts
Whenever a player maps a river in a new hex, he should check for the presence of cataracts. Roll 1 die: find a cataract on a roll of 1 if the hex contains jungle or veldt; on a roll of 1 or 2 if the hex contains desert; and on a roll of 2, 3, or 4 if the hex contains mountains. Cataracts will never be found in lake, swamp, or jungle-swamp hexes. Cataracts are always downstream from any branch that may occur in the hex.

When an expedition is following a river by canoe, it will have to portage around each cataract. If the expedition has less than eight bearers, it will not be able to portage around the cataract and will not be able to travel further along the river by canoe; the expedition may leave its canoes (in a cache) and continue along the river (or in any other direction allowed) on foot. When an expedition in canoes first discovers a cataract while going downstream, the player must roll one die, subtracting three, to determine the number of canoes (if any) which are lost by going over the falls.

Having found a cataract, the player will determine the height of the highest waterfall in it. Roll four dice and multiply them together for the height in feet. If the result is 400 or over, record it (in blue) on the hex for reference in scoring.

### Mountain Peaks
Whenever a player discovers mountain terrain in a hex, he will roll to determine the height of the most impressive peak. Roll three dice and multiply them together. Add 100 for the height of the peak in hundreds of feet. If the result is greater than 150 (15,000 feet) record it (in brown) on the hex for reference in scoring.

## Exploring: Discovery of Natives

The first time that each explorer enters an unpublished hex not already known to contain natives, the player will roll two dice to determine whether his explorer encounters any natives there.

| Hex Terrain | Find Natives on a Roll of: |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Lake** | 2 or 3 |
| **Veldt, Swamp, or Jungle** | 2, 3, or 4 |
| **Mountain** | 5 or 6 |
| **River or Oasis** | 7 |
*(Desert hexes have no natives without an oasis or a river.)*

### Number of Warriors
When natives are present, the number of warriors is given by the product of three dice (e.g., if the roll yields 2, 3, and 5, the result is 30 warriors). If the greatest die roll is larger than the distance (in hexes) to the nearest Port of Entry, use that distance in place of the greatest of the three die rolls (to simulate the depopulation caused by the Slave Trade). Place one of the numbered native markers on the hex, and record the details of the tribe for that number separately.

## Natives: Explorer Policy Towards Natives

Whenever an explorer enters a hex (or remains in a hex) known to contain natives or discovers natives, the player will indicate how his explorer will react to the natives. He has the following options:

1.  **Defensive Stand**: Remain in the hex, but go on the defensive. He may not hunt, engage in geological or zoological research, or trade with the natives. He may not leave the hex until his next turn.
2.  **Immediate Retreat**: Immediately leave the hex, retreating to the hex from which he has just come. He must remove his colored marker and, unless the hex has already been published, the player will lose all credit for exploring it. If he has movement left, he may move to some other hex from the hex into which he has retreated.
3.  **Cautious Show of Force**: Seek contact with the natives with great caution and a show of force.
4.  **Ignore Natives**: Continue his turn as though the natives were not there, hunting and doing geological or zoological research, or moving off to another hex (if he can otherwise move that fast).
5.  **Open Friendly Approach**: Approach the natives in an open, friendly manner.

---

## Natives: Native Reaction to Explorer Policy

First, determine the attitude of the natives. Roll three dice. Add the Distance (in hexes) from the nearest Port of Entry to the number of the policy selected by the explorer. 
*   If the explorer selected policy 4 or 5 and has no askaris, add two; if he is also unarmed add one.
*   If the explorer selected policy 3 or 5 and has a guide from an adjacent hex or has negotiated with this tribe before or if this hex has been published, add one.
*   If this tribe has been visited by previous expeditions, add or subtract the appropriate number for their Attitude Level.

If the result is greater than or equal to the die roll, the natives are not immediately hostile, and the expedition will not be attacked this turn. If either policy 3 or 5 was selected, the explorer may then negotiate with the chief (see *Negotiation*).

If the sum of the distance from the nearest Port of Entry and the adjusted explorer's policy is less than the roll of the dice, the natives are hostile. Their actions will then be based on the size of the expedition. Roll one die, multiply by the number of askaris, and divide by the explorer's policy number. If the result is larger than the number of warriors, the natives will hide rather than attack, and negotiation will be impossible. If the result is less than or equal to the number of warriors, they will attack. 

If the natives decide to attack, roll two dice to see if they surprise the expedition. If the expedition is not in the desert and is entirely mounted on camels and/or horses, add one. If it is in the desert and is not entirely mounted, subtract one. If the result is less than the explorer's policy number, the natives have surprised the expedition.

### Combat Resolution
*   **Expedition Not Surprised**: The askaris (and the explorer) will fire their muskets, each killing one warrior. Roll one die, multiply by the number of warriors killed; if the result is greater than the number of warriors remaining, the natives are driven away for the turn (see *Results of Victory*); if not, they close with the askaris and the combat continues. After the first shot by every askari, the battle will be too fierce to permit muskets to be reloaded, and the battle will continue in the same fashion as if the expedition had been surprised.
*   **Expedition Surprised**: No one will have a chance to shoot. Divide the number of attacking warriors by the roll of one die, dropping fractions, to obtain askari casualties. If all askaris are killed, go to *Consequences of Defeat*. If some askaris survive, multiply askari casualties by the roll of one die; if the result is greater than the original number of askaris at the start of the battle, they will break and run (go to *Consequences of Defeat*).
*   **Melee**: If the askaris hold their ground, divide the number of remaining askaris by the roll of one die (dropping fractions) and add one to obtain native casualties. Multiply the native casualties for this round by the roll of one die. If the result is greater than the number of remaining warriors, the natives have been defeated (go to *Results of Victory*); otherwise combat continues, with the askaris and the natives alternatively taking casualties and checking their reactions until one side is defeated or wiped out.

*   **2** — Explorer held prisoner.
*   **3** — Explorer escapes alone, without food or weapons.
*   **4** — Explorer escapes with musket and 9 rations.
*   **5** — Explorer escapes with musket, 9 rations, and one roll on the Escape Survival Table below.
*   **6 or more** — Explorer escapes with musket, 9 rations, and two rolls on the Escape Survival Table below.

*Note:* If a 5 or a 6 is rolled and the result of the roll is impossible (e.g., no camels in the expedition), roll again.

### Escape Survival Table:

2.  **Bearer** *
3.  **Askari**
4.  **Camel** *

*If one of the items marked with an asterisk is obtained, roll to see what it is loaded with:*

If the Explorer is captured, he must roll again every turn, subtracting three from his die roll. He remains in that hex until he is killed, escapes, or his player "retires" him and starts a new explorer.

## Natives: Results of Victory

Even if he was trying to run away, the explorer may now remain in the hex (and claim it if not already published). If askaris or bearers are deserting from the expedition for any reason, they will stop as soon as the Explorer gains a victory.

### Prisoners and Looting
Prisoners may be used as bearers or released. Roll one die and subtract it from the number of surviving askaris. The result is the number of prisoners taken, unless there were fewer surviving warriors, in which case all of them were taken prisoner. However, on a roll of six, the askaris do not take prisoners and will automatically loot the village, if it is found.

Add the explorer's policy number (1 to 5) to the number of prisoners and compare it to the roll of two dice. If the die roll is larger, the native village cannot be found (the explorer can remain in the hex and try again on succeeding turns). If the die roll is less than or equal to the number of prisoners plus the policy number, the village is located. The explorer may then:
1.  **Release the prisoners** and not loot the village, or
2.  **Keep the prisoners** as bearers and loot the village.

If he loots the village, roll 1 die per original warrior encountered: on a 1-4 find 1-4 rations of food; on a 5 find 1 gift; on a 6 find a camel (if in desert) or a canoe (if on a river, lake, or swamp). When desert tribes are defeated, one camel is also captured for every prisoner taken.

---

## Natives: Negotiation

When the Explorer seeks contact with the Natives (policies 3 and 5) and they are not hostile, the explorer may negotiate with the Chief.

The Explorer starts by offering the Chief gifts. Roll two dice. Multiply the number of gifts offered by the larger of the two die rolls. If the result is less than the number of warriors in the tribe, the Chief is insulted by the Explorer's lack of appreciation of his importance. The Chief becomes hostile and considers again whether to attack the expedition. If he does not feel strong enough to attack, he will attempt to poison the Explorer.

However, if the explorer gives the Chief everything he has (gifts, rations, muskets, horses, etc., except his men), the Chief will always be satisfied and will give the explorer 3-18 gifts (roll three dice) in return.

If the Chief is satisfied, the Explorer will obtain permission to evangelize, practice medicine, or trade with the Natives.

---

## Natives: Trading

Once the Explorer has received permission from the Chief to trade, he may use gifts or rations to buy supplies or animals or hire men for his expedition. Prices will vary from tribe to tribe and from turn to turn by die rolls for each item, as given below:

*   **Camel** (in desert hexes) — Sells for 1-6 gifts
*   **Canoe** (in swamp, lake, river hexes) — Sells for 1-6 gifts
*   **Horse** (in desert hexes) — Sells for 1-6 gifts
*   **Rations** — Sell for 1-6 rations per gift
*   **Bearers** — Hire for 1-6 gifts
*   **Guides** — Hire for 1-6 gifts
*   **Askaris** — Hire for 1-6 gifts and 1 musket per Askari

*Note on Trade Strategy:* Explorers may buy things one turn and sell them the next, making whatever profit is possible. Explorers without gifts can sometimes hunt, then sell extra rations to the Natives for gifts, and then buy needed equipment with the same gifts!

---

## Natives: Poisoning Explorer

Roll one die:
*   **1, 2** — Explorer is poisoned and dies.
*   **3** — Explorer is poisoned but recovers. (Add 1 to the die roll on any later poisoning attempts).
*   **4-6** — Attempt fails.

---

## Natives: Attitude Levels

After an explorer's first contact with the Natives in a hex, they will develop an attitude towards him and other explorers that will affect their possible hostility in later turns.

*   If he is defeated by them, but not killed, their attitude is reduced by one.
*   If he is killed, reduce their attitude by one more.
*   If he defeats them, there is no effect on their attitude, but if he takes prisoners and releases all of them, their attitude goes up by three (but only towards him, not towards other explorers).
*   Successes in medicine and/or evangelism also increase their attitude level and apply to all later explorers.

These levels are added to the Explorer's policy number in determining native reactions when explorers make later contacts with the tribe.

---

## Explorer Specialties

Whenever a player starts a new Explorer, he may choose to give him one, some, or all of these specialties: **Evangelism, Medicine, Zoology, and/or Geology**. Each specialty gives the Explorer additional opportunities to score points, but also imposes obligations which may waste his time or endanger his life. Once assigned a specialty, an Explorer cannot forget about it.

### Explorer Specialties: Evangelism
An explorer who has been designated as a missionary must attempt to evangelize each new tribe he encounters. Several successive missionaries can evangelize the same tribe, since not all missionaries are from the same church. After receiving permission from the chief, the Explorer will preach to the Natives with the following results (roll one die):

*   **1** — Natives jeer and taunt. No effect on attitude.
*   **2** — Natives ignore him. Attitude +1.
*   **3** — Natives listen respectfully. Attitude +2.
*   **4** — Natives applaud. Attitude +3. Chief and Witch Doctor each try to poison Explorer.
*   **5** — Witch Doctor asks to be baptised. Attitude +4. Chief tries to poison Explorer.
*   **6** — Chief asks to be baptised. Attitude +5.

*Preaching in Capture:* Missionary explorers who have been captured by Natives may also preach to them, without obtaining the chief's permission. If this results in an improvement in attitude in the tribe, it is added to the die roll (on the Consequences of Defeat Table) used to determine whether the explorer dies or escapes each turn. This may give results where the Explorer escapes with some Guides, Bearers, or Askaris—these being native converts who have chosen to go with him.

The results of an explorer's evangelism should be recorded for later use. The player will score up to five points per tribe, depending on the attitude change induced by his efforts.

### Explorer Specialties: Medicine
An explorer who has been designated a doctor must attempt to treat the ills of each tribe he encounters. After receiving permission from the chief, he practices medicine with the following results (roll one die):

*   **1 or less** — Explorer contracts strange disease and dies from it. No effect on Natives' attitude.
*   **2** — Explorer becomes sick (See *Sickness*). No effect on attitude.
*   **3** — Explorer fails to halt an epidemic. Chief attempts to poison him. Attitude -1.
*   **4** — No significant medical problems are available to be treated. No effect on attitude.
*   **5** — Explorer successfully treats many minor cases. Witch Doctor attempts to poison him. Attitude +1.
*   **6 or more** — Explorer successfully treats several major cases. Witch Doctor tries twice to poison him. Attitude +2.

Captured doctor-explorers may also attempt to practice medicine without obtaining permission from the Chief (subtract one from the die roll in determining results). Changes in attitude are added to the escape die roll. Doctor-explorers also enjoy an advantage in that they subtract one from the die roll when checking for medical disasters.

Doctors may score up to two points every turn that they treat any tribe (scoring 1 or 2 points on rolls of 5 or 6, respectively).

### Explorer Specialties: Zoology
An explorer who has been designated a zoologist must remain a turn in every unpublished hex he enters to look for interesting animal or plant life. Roll one die: find a useful specimen on a roll of:
*   **1** in a lake hex
*   **2 or 3** in a hex containing veldt or swamp
*   **2, 3, or 4** in a hex containing jungle
*   **4 or 5** in a hex containing mountains
*   **6** in a desert hex
*   **1** if a river or oasis is present

The Explorer is only allowed one discovery per hex. If he fails to make a discovery in his first attempt, he may leave. Specimens must be carried back to Europe as proof. One specimen may be carried in place of one food ration by a bearer or camel. An unlimited number of specimens may be carried by canoe.

### Explorer Specialties: Geology
An explorer who has been designated a geologist must remain in each unknown hex he enters for one turn to look for interesting mineral specimens. Roll one die: find a useful specimen on a roll of:
*   **1** in a swamp or jungle hex, without mountains
*   **1 or 2** in a jungle-mountain or veldt hex
*   **1-4** in a mountain hex, without jungle
*   **1-3** in a desert hex
*   **6** if a river is present

Geological specimens are never found in lake hexes. If the first attempt to find a specimen fails, the geologist need not remain in the hex again. Geological specimens must be taken back to Europe and are treated and carried the same as zoological ones.

*Specialty Conflict:* An explorer who is both a zoologist and a geologist cannot pursue both activities in the same turn. He will have to spend one turn working at each in each unpublished hex he enters.

---

## Hunting and Adjustment of Rations

Each man in the expedition will consume one food ration per turn. Before adjusting the markers on his Expedition Organization Chart, the player should determine whether the explorer and his askaris have shot any game to feed themselves. 

Roll one die, adjusting for terrain: **subtract 4 for desert, subtract 3 for lake, subtract 1 for swamp; add 1 for jungle, add 2 for river or oasis**. On a result of:
*   **1 or less** — No game at all.
*   **2 or 3** — Poor hunting; one ration per hunter.
*   **4 or 5** — Fair hunting; two rations per hunter.
*   **6 or more** — Excellent hunting; three rations per hunter.

*Zoologist Bonus:* Zoologist-explorers add one to the die roll, but only for their own results; askaris are not affected by the zoologist's bonus. Surplus game will not keep and cannot be saved.

---

## Starvation, Sickness, and Desertion

### Starvation
Starvation begins when there are not enough food rations left after hunting to feed all the men in the expedition. Camels and horses must be shot (providing 10 food rations each) until there is enough food for all the men. If there are still not enough food rations available, bearers will starve first, then guides, then askaris, and, finally, the explorer. 

*   **Starving guides** will desert (whether one posts guards or not).
*   **Starving askaris** will desert (taking their muskets with them).
*   **Starving bearers** will not carry, portage or paddle, and will desert unless there is an askari guarding each of them.
*   Starving men can walk, or ride in canoes (drifting or paddled by someone else). Starving explorers can do any normal task.

Each turn, roll one die for each starving man: on a roll of 4, 5, or 6, he becomes sick.

### Sickness
Sick men cannot walk and must be carried (by a horse or camel or two bearers, not carrying other loads, or in a canoe) or the expedition must halt. Roll one die each turn for each sick man: **subtract 1 if the explorer is a doctor and not himself sick, add 3 if the sick man is also starving**. 
*   **0 or 1** — The sick man recovers.
*   **5 or more** — The sick man dies.

### Thirst (Water Deprivation)
When there are not enough water rations left for all the men in the expedition which finds itself in a desert hex without a river or an oasis, guides will go thirsty first, then bearers, askaris, and, finally, the explorer. Animals and men without water rations simply die.

---

## Return to Europe

In order to score points, the explorer must return to Europe and "publish" his discoveries. To leave Africa, one must first reach a Port of Entry. The explorer will dismiss his surviving men, distributing his remaining supplies, animals, and equipment among them as a bonus. He will then leave for Europe, with a free passage home provided by the local Government (Governor, Consul, or Factor), regardless of how unsuccessful he has been. Explorers do not have to leave Africa by the same Port of Entry through which they entered.

---

## Publishing Discoveries

Explorers will be back in Europe on the turn following their departure from Africa. Their players will go back to drawing cards for donations for their next expeditions.

A player may claim as his discovery any of the hexes of Africa on which his colored markers rest, even if he was not the first explorer to enter that hex. Until he "publishes" a hex, any other player who has a colored marker on that hex may "steal" the hex for himself by publishing it first. There is no way for explorers to share the discovery of a single hex. 

When a hex has been published, the colored marker of the publishing player and the markers of any other players which rest on the published hex are removed. Hexes may only be published when the publishing player's explorer is in Europe. As each hex is published, the player will receive one extra donation card, and score the appropriate points. Published hexes are the only ones that count for scoring.

### Visited but Unpublished Hex Advantages:
Until a hex is published, the explorer who has visited it has some advantages over other explorers:
1.  He can move through it more rapidly than they can.
2.  He knows where the cataracts (if any) are and does not have to worry about losing canoes when he comes to them.
3.  He knows where the oases (if any) are.
4.  He knows whether the natives are friendly or hostile.
5.  He has a map of the area and is not likely to become lost.

When the hex is published, all explorers will be given this information, and the explorer who has visited it will lose his advantages there.

---

## Scoring

When an explorer dies or is retired, the player operating the explorer will score points according to the explorer's published discoveries. First, all markers of that player's color will be removed from the hexes the explorer has visited but not published. No new hexes may be published, unless the explorer is in Europe (in which case the player will probably publish every hex he can).

Next, erase all of the explorer's still unpublished hexes that have not been visited by any other explorer. Whatever terrain, natives, or other wonders the explorer may have discovered in these erased hexes are secrets that die with him.

### Points for Published Discoveries:

#### 1. Areas Mapped:
*   **Desert hex (hexagon)** — 1 point
*   **Veldt hex** — 1 point
*   **Lake hex** — 1 point
*   **River hex** — 1 point
*   **Jungle hex** — 2 points
*   **Swamp hex** — 2 points
*   **Oasis hex** — 2 points
*   *Composite Terrain:* Hexes with composite terrain gain points in all categories (e.g., swamp-jungle hexes count 4 points).

#### 2. Native Tribes:
*   **Discovered** — 1 point
*   **Defeated** — 1 point
*   **Doctored** — 1 or 2 points
*   **Evangelized** — 1 to 5 points according to results

#### 3. Geological or Zoological Discoveries:
*   **Specimens returned to Europe** — 1 point per specimen

#### 4. Superlative Discoveries:
*   **Highest mountain over 15,000 feet**: 1st: 8 pts, 2nd: 4 pts, 3rd: 2 pts, 4th: 1 pt
*   **Largest lake**: 1st: 4 pts, 2nd: 2 pts, 3rd: 1 pt
*   **Highest waterfall over 400 feet**: 1st: 4 pts, 2nd: 2 pts, 3rd: 1 pt
*   **Source of the longest river**: 1st: 16 pts, 2nd: 8 pts, 3rd: 4 pts, 4th: 2 pts, 5th: 1 pt
*(Only mountain peaks over 15,000 feet and waterfalls over 400 feet tall score points.)*

### Longest River Determination
In scoring for the discovery of the source of a river, that lake, swamp, jungle-swamp hex or river source which is furthest from the mouth of the river (counting the shortest possible route) will be determined. When several possible sources are the same distance from the mouth, the first of these to be published will be the "true" source, and the shortest distance from the mouth, following the river, will be the length of the river. The shortest path from the true source to the mouth is the main river; the shortest path from any other source to the point where it joins the main river is a tributary river.

In determining "the longest river in Africa," the Nile enjoys a considerable advantage, since there are 25 hexes of it off the map. Very rarely, however, the Congo (or some other river) will be discovered to be a single, long river, while the Nile spreads out into a huge area of swamps, lakes, and many short branches, which will allow the discovery of the source of the Congo to be more valuable than that of the Source of the Nile.

### Epic Transits
The first explorer to complete one of the following four crossings of the Continent will receive a bonus equal to the number of previously unpublished hexes through which the expedition has travelled in making the trip. The first explorer to do so will receive half a point per previously published hex passed through, the third one-third of a point, and the fourth one-fourth. Each explorer scoring points for such a crossing must use a different route and must publish every hex for which he scores the bonus. Only one explorer may score a bonus for any given crossing. While any of the trips may be made in either direction, the route may not pass through any of the pre-printed hexes at the northeast edge of the mapboard. The following are the crossings for which bonuses may be scored:
1.  **Between Khartoum and any of the ports of entry in Cape Colony** (Capetown, Port Elizabeth, Durban).
2.  **Between Khartoum and any of the East Coast ports of entry north of Durban**.
3.  **Between Khartoum and any of the West Coast ports of entry north of Capetown**.
4.  **Between any West Coast port of entry north of Capetown and any East Coast port of entry north of Durban**.

## Superlative Geographic Features at the Start of the Game

*   A number of mountains in the Atlas and Drakensberg ranges were "known" to be about 15,000 feet high in 1820. (These all were proved, in the 20th Century, to be errors in measurement!)
*   No large lakes were known to exist in Africa in 1820.
*   Several waterfalls of approximately 400 feet were known.
*   The five longest rivers known in Africa by 1820 were: the **White Nile** (33 hexes, source not known), the **Niger** (23 hexes), the **Orange** (12 hexes), the **Senegal** (10 hexes), and the **Gambia** (7 hexes).
